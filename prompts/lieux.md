- **Cathédrale Saint-Benoît** : the cathedral of Castres, BAROQUE, built 1678–1718: a massive plain classical stone façade, a single wide nave under a low tiled roof, and beside it an older square stone bell tower. NOT Gothic — no spires, no pinnacles, no flying buttresses.
- **Hôtel de Ville** : the former bishop's palace (1666–1675, designed by Jules Hardouin-Mansart), now town hall and Goya museum: a long symmetrical classical stone façade of two storeys with tall regular windows and a central entrance, opening onto a formal garden.
- **Musée Goya** : same building as the town hall, the former bishop's palace by Hardouin-Mansart.
- **Jardin de l'Évêché** : formal French garden attributed to Le Nôtre, beside the bishop's palace: clipped box parterres in scrolling patterns, gravel paths, trimmed trees.
- **Pont Vieux** : the old stone arch bridge over the Agout: several round arches, low stone parapets.
- **Pont Neuf** : stone arch bridge over the Agout with round arches and stone parapets.
- **Agout** : the river Agout: calm water between stone quays. On its west bank between the two bridges stand the famous old houses of the tanners and weavers. They rise STRAIGHT OUT OF THE WATER on a continuous base of rough stone pierced by ROUND VAULTED ARCHES at water level. Above that base the façades are of red brick and painted plaster, three or four storeys, and their upper floors are almost entirely GLAZED WOODEN GALLERIES — deep verandas of small panes in painted timber frames, projecting over the water, one above the other, each house painted a different colour. Low tiled roofs, some with an open drying loft under the eaves. (Verified on a photograph.)
- **Place Jean Jaurès** : the main square of Castres, and — unlike the rest of the old town — a 19th-CENTURY ensemble, so draw it as described here and not as a medieval street. Its ground is bare paving with nothing built on it. Every building whose façade meets this square has, from the bottom up: a GROUND-FLOOR ARCADE OF ROUND STONE ARCHES running the full width of the building, arch after arch without interruption, with the shops and café terraces set back under the arches and fabric awnings hung inside them; then two storeys of pale rendered stone with tall rectangular windows and moulded surrounds, the first floor carrying a CONTINUOUS CAST-IRON BALCONY along the whole façade; then a heavy moulded cornice on corbels; then a STEEP MANSARD ROOF of dark slate pierced by a row of dormer windows. One of these buildings carries a large clock in an ornate carved cartouche above the cornice. Which buildings face the square, how many there are, how long they are and what shape the square itself has: all of that is read from image 3, never invented. (Verified on a photograph of the square.)
- **Statue de Jean Jaurès** : a standing figure of Jaurès in PALE WHITE STONE — not bronze — bearded, in a frock coat, one hand at his waist. He stands on a tall square pedestal of grey granite bearing a small bronze plaque, itself on a wide stepped granite base, the whole set on a low raised platform of polished granite slabs surrounded by a SHALLOW SHEET OF WATER: the fountain and the statue are one and the same object, not two. (Verified on a photograph.)
- **Quai des Jacobins** : the quay street running along the west bank of the Agout, its parapet overlooking the water.
- **Théâtre Municipal** : the municipal theatre of 1904, an ornate Belle Époque façade on the place de la République.
- **Hôtel de Nayrac** : a 16th-century Renaissance mansion in brick and stone with corner pavilions and mullioned windows.
- **Hôtel de Viviès** : a 17th-century stone mansion with a large carriage porch and a courtyard.
- **Grand temple** : the Protestant temple, a plain classical façade with a pediment.
- **Rue Vieille Halle** : a narrow paved lane of the old town, granite setts, running down to the square. Its houses are three or four storeys of pale ashlar stone laid in visible courses, their GROUND FLOORS pierced by tall round-arched openings closed with plain iron railings or glazed shopfronts set back in the arch. The upper windows are plain rectangles with moulded stone surrounds and no shutters, some with a small iron balconette. Roofs are low-pitched, in red-brown canal tile. Wall-mounted lanterns on iron brackets at first-floor level, awnings over the ground-floor shops. (Verified on Street View.)
